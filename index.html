        The loop

        Mission Control can be 3 to 22 minutes away by light, and the link can drop out entirely. So HealthLoop makes every health decision on the spacecraft first, tells the crew member what it means and what to do, then sends a small, encrypted summary to Earth.

        1 · SenseHeart rate, HRV, SpO₂, stress score every 10 min. Sleep each morning. Daily self check-in.
        2 · CompareEach reading is checked against that astronaut's own baseline, separately for sleep and waking hours.
        3 · Act onboardPlain-language alert with steps to take now. No waiting for Earth.
        4 · Pack and sealBinary records (7 bytes per person), batched every 30 min, AES-GCM encrypted.
        5 · PrioritiseAlerts jump the queue. If bandwidth runs short, old routine telemetry is dropped first. Alerts are never dropped.
        6 · Review and acknowledgeThe flight surgeon sees status with its data age, and the acknowledgment travels back with the same delay.

        How an alert fires: one heart-rate reading, traced

        Smallest example: Mateo's waking heart rate. His baseline is learned from his own last 14 days, not a population average.

        Quick check: if Mateo's HR reads 71 three times in a row (z ≈ 2.7), what level does he get? Answer: WATCH, because z is above 2.5 but below 3.5.

        Built on NASA research

        The challenge names the dangers of long missions: radiation, isolation and confinement, altered gravity and a hostile closed environment. We used NASA’s own frameworks to decide what HealthLoop watches, and NASA’s published Earth–Mars delay to model the link.

        Design decisions

        • Onboard-first. The challenge says astronauts carry the responsibility for spotting changes. Every alert gives the crew member next steps before Earth even knows.
        • Personal baselines, split by sleep and wake. A heart rate of 52 is normal asleep and odd awake. Population thresholds would either miss real changes or alert constantly.
        • Bandwidth. A 30-minute telemetry batch for 4 crew is about 64 bytes encrypted, versus roughly 500 bytes as JSON. Watch the counters on the Mission Control tab.
        • Delayed comms. Mission Control always sees the data age. During a blackout, alerts are stored and forwarded when the link returns.
        • Security. Every packet is sealed with AES-GCM, which encrypts and detects tampering. A fresh random nonce is used per packet. In this demo the key is generated in your browser and never leaves it.
        • A language model, kept in its lane. The detector decides every alert level, because it is tested and predictable. The model only explains and summarises: it answers crew questions from their own live readings, turns free-text check-ins into a few bytes of structured data, and writes the flight surgeon's handover. On the ship it runs as a local model through Ollama, since there is no internet in deep space; on Earth it can use Claude. If neither is running, built-in rules answer instead.
        • Limits. This is a hackathon prototype with simulated data, not a medical device. Real use would need validated sensors, flight-surgeon-approved thresholds and key management on certified hardware.
